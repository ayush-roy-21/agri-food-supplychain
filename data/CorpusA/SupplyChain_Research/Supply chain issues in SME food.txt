Supply chain issues in SME food
sector: a systematic review
Arun Jose and PrasannaVenkatesan Shanmugam
Department of Production Engineering,
National Institute of Technology, Tiruchirappalli, India
Abstract
Purpose – The purpose of this paper is to investigate the significant supply chain issues in the small and
medium-sized enterprise (SME) food industry. The objectives are to identify the major themes and the
dynamic evolution of SME food supply chain (FSC) issues, the current research trends, the different modelling
approaches used in SME FSC, and the most addressed SME food sector.
Design/methodology/approach – In all, 3,733 published articles from 2002 to 2018 in the Clarivate
Analytics Web of Science database were collected, from which 1,091 articles were shortlisted for the review.
The authors used bibliographic coupling combined with co-word analysis to identify the historical relations of
the research themes that emerged during the periods 2002 – 2014 and 2002 – 2018.
Findings – This research identified five major research themes such as production and distribution in
alternative food networks, relationship, safety and standards in the FSC, greenhouse gas (GHG) emission
impact of the farm food system, traceability and product quality in FSC and asymmetric price transmission in
the FSC. Among the identified themes, GHG emission impact of the farm food system and traceability and
product quality in the FSC have received increasing attention in recent years. The dairy sector is the most
addressed sector (36 per cent), followed by fruits and vegetables (27 per cent), meat and poultry (18 per cent),
seafood (10 per cent) and grains and oilseed (8 per cent). It is also identified that the dairy sector has received
significant attention in the “GHG Emission impact of farm food system ” theme. Similarly, meat and poultry
sectors have received much attention in the “Traceability and product quality in the food supply chain ”
theme. Also, the authors identified that the empirical modelling approaches are the most commonly used
solution methodology, followed by the conceptual/qualitative methods in the SME FSC.
Originality/value – This study maps and summarizes the existing knowledge base of supply chain issues in the
SME food sector. The results of this review provide the major research areas, most commonly used approaches and
food sectors addressed. This study also highlights the research gaps and potential future research direction.

1. Introduction
The food supply chain (FSC) technically spans the entire“farm-to-fork” process. A typical FSC
includes farmer, marketer who supplies produc ts to food processors/producers, wholesaler,
retailers/caterer and consumer (Mattevi and Jones, 2016a). However, the exact supply chain path
for a particular food product depends on the product characteristics, size and market power of
the supply chain members (Dani and Deep, 2010). Managing FSC is complex as compared to
other supply chains due to the factors like the freshness of food, quality, safety, etc. (Zhonget al.,
2017). A major portion of the food industries pa rticipating in the FSC comprises of small and
medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) (Mertins et al., 2012). The annual statistics of Food Drink
Europe (2016) shows that about 99.1 per cent of all food processing industries in the EU are
SMEs. The food processing SMEs comprise dairy, beverages, food chains, spice trading, meat,
fruits, vegetables, etc. In most of the developing countries, including India, food SMEs provide
not only huge employment opportunities but they also ensure regional balance by taking
industrialization to rural and backward areas.The evaluation and advancement of FSCs from
SMEs are essential, from the point of view of SMEperformance and for the health of the society
(Henchion and McIntyre, 2005). With the increasingdigital technologies, designing/restructuring

2.2 Locating studies and evaluation
The articles for the study are identified and screened after formulating the research questions.
This section summarizes the steps followed for locating the study and its evaluation.
2.2.1 Time horizon . Articles published during 2002 – 2018 were considered for the study.
The works prior to 2002 were excluded due to the limited number of publications on the
supply chain issues in SME food sectors (Mahajan et al., 2017; Siddh et al., 2015). Being the
immediate previous year at the time of the review, we included all articles till the end of
the year 2018.
2.2.2 Selection of database . WoS database from Clarivate Analytics is used to collect the
articles. We selected the WoS database for the following solid reasons. The first reason is to
get a comprehensive result and to ensure the quality of articles. The second WoS database
includes more than 18,000 journals of high impact from six different citation databases.
Third, the retrieved result from WoS can be organized and analysed easily through
bibliometric software tools (Colicchia and Strozzi, 2012).
2.2.3 Search criteria for article selection . As proposed by Fahimnia et al. (2015), a three-
level keyword was used to extract articles as shown in Table II. “OR” connectors were used
to connect keywords in each level, and different levels of keywords were connected by using
“AND” connectors. To find root word plus, we used “*” symbol at the end of some words.
Different terms with their variants were used at each level of the search strings so as to not
limit the database search to a specific aspect of the research domain, as our primary
objective was to identify the major research concepts. For example, Level 1 keywords used
the term “supply chain ” including its variants such as “inventory”, “logistics”, “supply
network” or “value chain ”.
2.2.4 Sorting of articles . In order to include the most relevant articles, a set of inclusion
and exclusion criteria is defined, followed by Newbert (2007) as listed below:
(1) peer-reviewed scientific journals published only in English are included;
(2) articles published during 2002 – 2018 were considered for the study since articles that
met our selection criteria before 2002 were very limited;
(3) selected articles that contain at least one keyword in each level of the search string in
the title, abstract and keywords;
(4) commercial publications, book chapters, proceedings papers, periodicals and
working papers are not included; and
(5) articles that are not relevant to the SME FSC are excluded.
During the initial search, 3,733 articles are retrieved from the WoS database, and after
applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria (1 – 4), a total of 3,040 articles are retained. Both
the authors independently screened the papers on the basis of the title, abstract and
keywords. The papers for which both the authors agreed have been included. In case of any
discrepancy, we included a paper for full-text reading. Specifically, we excluded 1,949

2.3 Bibliographic coupling (BC) network and cluster analysis
Bibliometric and network analysis tools are used in a systematic review to identify and
cluster significant research concepts. A bibliometric network is a weighted network
consisting of nodes and edges. The node represents a knowledge actor like a research paper,
authors, journal, authors ’ institute/country or keywords. The edge between two nodes
denotes the relationship between them. The five commonly studied edge relationship
includes citation, co-citation, BC, co-author and co-word analysis (Zupic and Cater, 2015). A
BC connects journals, authors or documents with respect to the number of shared
references. Among the bibliometric networks, BC could capture the research dynamics from
past to present, as it includes the most recent contributions (Vogel and Güttel, 2013). The
bibliographic strength between the two articles is defined by the number of items shared by
these two articles in their reference records (Zhao and Strotmann, 2008). Boyack and
Klavans (2010) compared the accuracy of co-citation analysis, BC, direct citation and found
that BC outperforms the other two. In literature, bibliographic analysis tools like CiteSpace,
Pajek, Bibexcel, R, SciMat and Saint are proposed for bibliometric analysis (Cobo and
Herrera, 2011). Among these, BibExcel allows processing the different types of file formats
like Medline format, Scopus RIS format and the WoS plain text format (Persson et al., 2009).
Also, Bibexcel allows data modification or filtering of imported details from various
databases such as WoS and Scopus (Bjurström, 2011). Due to these advantages, BibExcel is
used in this research for the bibliometric analysis.
In order to conduct BC, the plain text format from WoS containing the details of author,
the title of publication, year of publication, abstract and authors affiliation from the
shortlisted 1,091 articles are exported to Bibexcel software. To further map and visualize,
the BC among the 1,091 articles network and cluster analysis tools are used. In literature,
UCINET, Mathematica, Pajek, VOS Viewer and Gephi are the commonly used network
analysis tools. Among these, Gephi is generally used in research projects, given its
simplicity (Fahimnia et al., 2015). Gephi is capable of handling large network data and helps
in filtering, navigating, manipulating and clustering the data. It can also handle a variety of
graph data sets generated in comma-separated values files, Graph and Net file formats. In
this research, the shared references from Bibexcel in .net format were imported to Gephi and
were used for networking and clustering the research articles.
To identify the sub-fields/themes of a research domain, the nodes of a network are
divided into clusters or modules using clustering techniques (Fahimnia et al. , 2015). The
degree of association is strong among members of the same cluster and weak between
members of different clusters. For dividing the network into clusters, the default Louvain
clustering algorithm in the Gephi software is used, which will maximize the modularity
index by iterating each node to all clusters (Rajagopal et al. , 2017). Modularity is an
important metric that provides information about the overall clustering of the networks.
The modularity index is a scalar value between −1 and +1 that measures the density of
links inside the clusters vs the links between the clusters (Fahimnia et al., 2015).

2.4 Text mining (TM)
To find out the research focus of each sub-field, the contents of either all or the lead papers
in each sub-field are analysed and evaluated. The interpretation and analysis of sub-field
depend greatly on subjective factors and require expertise in the specific research field. To
avoid the reviewer bias and for a deeper understanding of the concepts addressed in each
sub-field, researchers have further used text mining (TM) as a content analysis technique to
retrieve the co-occurred words from the documents and to build a conceptual structure of
the domain (Ghadge et al., 2012; Song and Chambers, 2014). In this research, the co-occurred
words from the research articles in each sub-field are retrieved using TM to identify
research concept precisely. Co-word analysis presumes that a group of aggregated terms/
words could indicate the underlying themes and that co-occurrences of terms/words could
show the associations with the underlying themes. Title and abstract of all articles in each
sub-field are downloaded from the WoS database in .txt format and text mined separately
using Standford NLP core 3.6.0. StanfordcoreNLP 3.6.0 software is used in this research for
TM since it is flexible and simple (Song and Chambers, 2014).
Using Stanford metrics conversion Handler Programme, the output extensible markup
language file from the Standford NLP is converted to a GraphML network file from the
identified co-occurred words. The GraphML file is then used as input in Gephi for visualization
and further analysis of the co-word network. After establishing a co-word network, the
research concept of each cluster is then identified by measuring the relative frequency of these
co-occurred words and by measuring the network properties based on node and network-level
metrics (Barbara and Wichmann, 2016). The node-level metrics can be divided into local:
degree centrality, and the ones relative to the rest of the network (i.e. based on node’s location
within a network): closeness, betweenness and eigenvector centrality (Milojevic, 2013). The
number of direct links between a node and other nodes in the cluster is defined by degree
centrality. A word with a higher degree will be in the centre of the network and considered as
the information hub. The closeness centrality is defined by the inverse of the average length of
the shortest paths to or from all the other nodes in the network. Higher closeness centrality
refers to higher influence on other nodes. The betweenness centrality is a measure of how often
a node is located on the shortest path between other nodes in the network. Eigenvector
centrality is based on the principle that importance of a node in a network may increase with
having links to the other nodes that are themselves important. It is calculated by providing a
relative score for each node proportional to the sum of the scores of its neighbours (Bonacich,
2007). The equations used to calculate the node centrality are given in Appendix 1.

3.3.2 Cluster 2: relationship, safety and standards in food supply chain . The relationship
management includes the process of developing profound and long-standing relationships
with consumers and suppliers through constructing links between the supply chain
members (Fredriksson and Liljestrand, 2015). Although a number of research works
are conducted on supply chain relationship in the food sector, the majority of them are
focussing on the farmer – retailer or farmer – processor relationships. The relationship
issues are further classified as contract-based relationship, a relationship grounded on trust
and communication.
It is evident that contractual relationship among the supply chain members will decrease the
transaction costs and market imperfections (Neilson, 2007), and intensify access to marketing
(Blandon, 2010). Trust is considered as the soul of supply chain management (Fu et al., 2017a),
and it is the inter-personal belief acquired from the experience by enabling a safe and secure food
supply (Fischer, 2013). According to Fu et al. (2017a), a trust-based relationship among the
supply chain members can stimulate the information sharing and corporation. A successful SC
relationship is defined as a collaborative relati onship, characterized by a high level of trust
and communication ( Jonsson and Gustavsson, 2008). The quality of a food sector depends not
only on its own quality but also on the quality of its suppliers, and hence the information
sharing between the firms and their suppliers have an important role in the FSC (Huoet al., 2018).
Apart from this, the unclear data about the quality of the product will lead to a crisis, and

3.3.3 Cluster 3: GHG emission impact of the farm food system . The agricultural
production has developed significantly over the recent decades, resulting in the increased use
of fertilisers and mechanization that lead to the environmental consequences (Tasca et al. ,
2017). To assess the environmental impacts of food systems, various metrics have been
established that include the quantification of GHG emissions and water usage of food items,
assessment of water footprint and food production systems through LCA (Green et al., 2018).
Here, we classified the food emission according to GHG emission and water footprints. GHGs
can be explained with different types of emissions including carbon dioxide (CO
2), methane
(CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O) (Vida et al., 2017; Christie et al., 2011). The food products in the
supply chain produce GHG emission from the farm-to-fork through manufacturing,
distribution and even retailing (Dorward, 2012). Water pollution and consumption at a global
scale are recognized as the key resource use issues. Agricultural production is the foremost
water user, and the major water footprint approaches applied to agricultural commodities are
the water footprint network method and LCA method (Zonderland-Thomassen et al., 2012).
The water footprint is characterized by green, blue and grey water along the whole life cycle of
a product (Payen et al., 2018). The green water footprint is the volume of water evaporated
from the root zone of the soil, and it is particularly relevant for forestry, horticultural and
agricultural products. The blue water footprint is the volume of freshwater that evaporated
from surface or groundwater resources to produce goods and services. Domestic water use,
irrigated agriculture and industry can each have a blue water footprint. Grey water footprint is
the amount of freshwater required to dilute a load of pollutants to meet the specific water
quality standards.
Our result indicates that 90 per cent of the research is on GHG emission studies and the
water footprint issues need further attention. The conceptual methodologies have been
widely used, whereas the empirical models and quantitative techniques investigating
emission from the food products and its impact are limited and need further attention.
Besides, our result indicates that the dairy sector is the most addressed food sector in this
research theme, whereas research on fruits and vegetables, meat and poultry and seafood
sector is limited.

3.3.4 Cluster 4: traceability and product quality in the food supply chain . Olsen and Borit
(2013) defined traceability as the ability to access information related to things that are to be
considered throughout their whole life cycle using recorded identification. The term
“Traceability” includes both “tracing” and “tracking” (Dandage et al. , 2017). Tracing is a
reverse process in which origin is identified in the supply chain, and tracking is a forward
process where the end users are identified by the location in the supply chain.
The articles in this cluster are divided into three main sub-themes such as quality and
safety, sustainability and cost-related issue. Traceability is recognized as a mechanism for
quality and safety (Manzini et al., 2013), improving sustainability (Giagnocavo et al., 2017;
Mattevi and Jones, 2016a) and reducing the overall cost (Dandage et al. , 2017) of the food
product since it reduces the recall ’s probability. Dabbene et al. (2014) identified many issues
related to traceability of bulk products, fraud prevention, quality and identity preservation
concerns and food crisis management. For the past few years, many developed and
developing countries, for instance, Austria, Germany, the UK, etc., have made it mandatory
to implement food traceability systems (Riviere and Buckley, 2012). However, it is
surprising that India, the second largest producer of fruits and vegetables in the world
(Saxena and Gandhi, 2014), does not have any required traceability system (Schroeder and
Tonsor, 2012).
In recent years, an increasing effort in the implementation of traceability systems can be
seen, mainly as a result of a growing concern with food quality and safety issues (Saltini
et al. , 2013). An optimized and sustainable supply chain necessarily requires taking into
account the traceability (Germani et al. , 2015). To overcome the food quality and safety
issues, it is necessary to develop a trusted food traceability system, which can track and
monitor the complete lifespan of food production (Lin et al. , 2018). Of late, McDonald
restaurants presented the food traceability system to monitor the item sources from various
providers of the nation, keeping in mind the end goal to give food security and quality
(Dandage et al. , 2017). For this, a mobile application called TrackMyMacca ’s was created.
Through this application, the global positioning system (GPS) provides the location of the
restaurant, the tracking image on the McDonald ’s packaging is recognized by the software,
and combining these data along with date and time, the application accesses McDonald ’s
supply database to get the story of the products. Proper traceability in the food industry is
required due to the precarious nature of the existing supply chain and to reduce the
numerous cases of food safety incidents and fraudulence (Dandage et al., 2017). Improving
sustainability in the SME food sector usually leads to a higher cost. However, by creating
transparency through the traceability, consumers have to be convinced that often higher
prices are involved to improve sustainability (Khan et al., 2018). A smart traceability system
in the SME food sector can be built by the application of modern technologies such as cloud
computing, GPS, Internet of Things (IoT), Big Data, artificial intelligence (AI), etc., into
traditional agriculture. Lin et al. (2018) conducted a study on the influence of Blockchain and
IoT technologies on the agri-food sector and found that these technologies help to build a
self-organized, open, trusted and ecological smart agriculture system, which includes all
parties in the system, even if they may not trust each other.
From the analysis, it is observed that studies on the impact of traceability on
sustainability and cost of the product need further attention. Developing smart traceability
system in the SME food sector is a ripe research area. From Table X, it is observed that
empirical and quantitative models have received much attention. Our results indicate that
meat and poultry and fruits and vegetables sectors are the most addressed food sector in
this cluster.

3.4 Research implications
The food industry represents one of the largest manufacturing sectors in the global
economy. FSC has been coined to depict the activities or operations from production,
distribution, and consumption so as to keep the safety and quality of various food products
under efficient and effective modes (Blandon et al. , 2009b). A major portion of the food
processing and distribution industries participating in the FSC comprises of SMEs.
Implementing SCM practices to improve the operations in food SMEs is not as easy as in
bigger counterparts, as there may be lots of complexities and difficulties in analysing the
operational objectives (Nabhani and Shokri, 2009). Indeed, both academics and practitioners
pay increasing attention towards the supply chain issues in food SMEs. Despite this
growing attention, the extant literature has yet to provide any evidence of a systematic
review of supply chain issues in SME food sector. To address this research gap, we present
an SLR of supply chain issues in SME food sector on the basis of BC and co-word analysis.
The findings of this research exhibit the following implications for research, practice, and
society. The policy makers could use the findings for developing strategies for the
betterment of SMEs in the FSC, which will provide huge employment opportunities and
ensure regional balance by taking industrialization to rural and backward areas.
Our review contributes to the body of FSC literature by reporting the major supply chain
issues/themes, the current research trends and the different modelling approaches used in
the SME food industry. We have identified five major themes, of which two themes, namely,
GHG emission impact of the farm food system and traceability and product quality in the
FSC, have received increasing attention in recent years. Our findings support the recent
study (Mattevi and Jones, 2016a) that suggests managers should see “being traceable” as a
strategic objective as it is “being green”, and it further reports that traceability could follow
the sustainability development path, due to consumer pressure on traceability. Food
traceability is one of the main factors in the evaluation of quality assurance systems in the

4. Conclusion
This paper presents an SLR and a comprehensive analysis of 1,091 articles in supply chain
issues in SME food sector from 2002 to 2018. To understand the dynamic evolution, we
adopted a comparative approach to the analysis of the data, which we divided into two
roughly equal parts. We used BC combined with co-word analysis to identify the historical
relations of the research themes that emerged during the periods 2002 – 2014 and 2002– 2018.
The broader thematic context is obtained for the period 2002 – 2014, whereas the inclusion of
recent articles (i.e. 2015– 2018) resulted in identifying the emerging research directions out of
these broader issues. From the results of bibliographic network and co-word analysis on the
data, we detected five major themes as production and distribution in AFN, relationship,
safety and standards in FSC, GHG emission impact of farm food system, traceability and
product quality in FSC and asymmetric price transmission in the FSC. It is identified that
GHG emission and traceability themes have received increasing attention in recent years. It
is observed that “SME” is associated with all the four clusters except the asymmetric price
transmission, indicating the association of this keyword with the corresponding clusters.
The research articles are further analysed with reference to the research focus, modelling
approaches used and food sectors addressed. The major food sector addressed in the SME
FSC is the dairy sector, followed by fruits and vegetables, meat and poultry, seafood sector
and grain and oil seeds, respectively. The most commonly used modelling techniques are
empirical methodology/case study, followed by conceptual or qualitative method, and
review; however, quantitative method needs further attention.
